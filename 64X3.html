Response · Reviewer 64X3 · Novelty

What does event supervision add beyond Robometer and WVM?

Critical Weaknesses
The core ingredients are individually close to existing work: Robometer already combines temporal progress labels from demonstrations with supervision from real failures (success prediction and trajectory preferences), and WVM's Suboptimal-Value-Bench already converts annotated hesitation/retry intervals into reference progress structure. The contribution reduces mainly to replacing trajectory-level preferences with event-anchored within-trajectory margin constraints, which is a sensible but incremental delta.

Response

We agree that using successful demonstrations and non-ideal executions to train reward models has precedents. Our core contribution is to directly supervise the progress predictions used to compute shaping rewards through progress relations between states before and after events. For real suboptimal and failed executions, it is difficult to label the task progress of each state. For example, when a robot picks up a cup and then drops it, it is difficult to determine the progress value to assign after the drop, but we can determine that progress is lower after the drop than before it. Event2Reward uses these relations directly as supervision and combines them with progress targets from successful demonstrations to train the model, without assigning specific progress values to these states in suboptimal and failed executions or prescribing complete progress curves for them.

Comparison with Robometer

Robometer learns from real failed executions through success prediction and trajectory preferences, but this supervision does not directly identify which transitions within an execution should be penalized. In the cup example above, even if the robot picks up the cup again and completes the task, preferring this successful trajectory over a failed one does not identify the drop as a transition to penalize. Since shaping rewards depend on differences between adjacent progress predictions, Ft=γpt+1−ptF_t=\gamma p_{t+1}-p_t, Event2Reward directly constrains post-drop progress to be lower than pre-drop progress, providing the local supervision needed for negative shaping feedback at the drop.

The video comparison (Figure R3) shows a bottle being dropped and then picked up again: Event2Reward’s progress prediction decreases after the drop and rises after re-grasping, while Robometer’s prediction does not clearly reflect the loss of progress caused by the drop. Robometer’s incorrect progress predictions produce incorrect reward feedback, encouraging the policy to learn incorrect actions. Our experiments also show that Event2Reward performs better in downstream policy learning (Table 9).

Comparison with WVM

WVM’s Suboptimal-Value-Bench constructs reference progress curves from annotated hesitation and retry intervals. These curves not only indicate that a regression occurred, but also specify how much progress decreases and how it subsequently recovers. In real executions, however, these changes depend on what the robot accomplishes. For example, after a bottle is dropped, it may remain on the table even as the arm keeps moving, so progress should not recover merely with the passage of time. Event2Reward directly supervises the model to predict lower progress after the drop than before it, and an increase after re-grasping. We do not need to assign a progress value to each of these states or prescribe a curve for the recovery.

This video illustration (Figure R4) makes the distinction concrete. After the bottle drops, the reference curve constructed using WVM’s rule returns to its pre-drop value at 10.3 seconds, but the bottle is still on the table and the robot has not yet picked it up again. Event2Reward’s progress prediction decreases after the drop and rises during re-grasping and lifting.

In addition, the reference curves in WVM’s Suboptimal-Value-Bench end at task completion, whereas our event supervision does not require an execution to ultimately succeed. Even if the robot never re-grasps the dropped cup and ultimately fails to complete the task, we can still determine that progress is lower after the drop than before it and use this relation to train the progress model. The real-robot rollout dataset we constructed contains 46,958 failed executions, accounting for 64.7% of all trajectories (Appendix A.1, Table 2). WVM cannot use these failed executions for training through the reference-curve supervision described above, whereas Event2Reward can use their event relations to train the progress model. Beyond the supervision method itself, we also define four types of task-progress events and construct a corresponding event-annotated dataset, enabling progress relations in real suboptimal and failed executions to be systematically annotated and used for reward-model training.

Response · Reviewer 64X3 · Robometer comparison

Why does Event2Reward outperform Robometer?

Critical Weaknesses · 10
The paper reports that Event2Reward outperforms Robometer but provides little analysis of why, given that Robometer also leverages progress and preference signals from real failures.
Questions For Authors · 9
Why does Event2Reward outperform Robometer, which also exploits progress and preference signals from real failures? Can the authors provide a more detailed mechanistic analysis?

Response

A key reason is that Event2Reward directly corrects the progress predictions used to compute shaping rewards. Robometer uses a preference head to compare entire executions and a separate progress head to predict task progress. Although the two heads share a backbone, the preference loss does not directly penalize local errors in the progress head. Thus, even when the model correctly ranks a successful trajectory above a failed one, it may still mistake a drop for progress. Event2Reward instead directly constrains post-drop progress to be lower than pre-drop progress and penalizes predictions that violate this relation.

The video example (Figure R5) illustrates this difference. The bottle drops onto the table and is later picked up again. Robometer’s progress predictions do not clearly distinguish the regression after the drop from the subsequent recovery. Event2Reward predicts a clear decrease after the drop and an increase as the robot re-grasps and lifts the bottle. This example illustrates that the ability to compare entire executions does not guarantee correct progress feedback during an execution.

The supervision ablations in §4.1 and Appendix C.1 further show that learning to compare trajectories does not necessarily yield more effective shaping rewards. Progress + Preference, which uses Robometer-style trajectory-comparison learning, achieves an average accuracy of 95% on unseen success–failure comparisons in LIBERO-10 (Table 10), confirming that it learned the comparison task. However, the results in §4.1 show a normalized evaluation AUC of 36.7 ± 7.8% for Progress + Relation versus 19.4 ± 14.3% for Progress + Preference (Figure 3 and Table 9). Progress-only achieves 19.6 ± 10.1% (Table 9). These results show that, in this experiment, despite achieving 95% trajectory-comparison accuracy, Progress + Preference performs similarly to Progress-only in policy learning, while Progress + Relation, which directly supervises progress relations, performs better.

In addition to the experiments in §4.1 of the main paper, we provide results on all ten LIBERO-Object tasks to examine the same difference in supervision on another suite. Each condition uses five environment seeds. Within 200k environment steps, Progress + Relation achieves 57.76 ± 6.59% evaluation success, compared with 44.28 ± 9.04% for Progress + Preference, 48.24 ± 7.35% for Progress-only, and 41.36 ± 4.08% for Relation-only (Figure R1).

The comparison between the full Event2Reward and Robometer systems is reported in §4.2.

Response

The model predicts current task progress from the task instruction and visual observations. Temporal labels from successful demonstrations tell the model approximately how far the task has progressed at a given state. Event annotations specify how progress should relate across states. Both types of supervision train the same progress model, through progress cross-entropy and relation penalties, respectively. We have added the relevant definitions to §3.3 of the revised manuscript and explain the two components and their aggregation below.

Soft encoding

The model outputs probabilities over 101 progress values, {0,0.01,…,1}\{0,0.01,\ldots,1\}, whose probability-weighted average is the predicted progress. For a temporal label yty_t from a successful demonstration, we encode this single progress value as a probability distribution over nearby progress values and train the model using cross-entropy against it. This target distribution is constructed using discrete Gaussian kernels with width σ=2\sigma=2 bins and mean progress equal to yty_t. We have clarified this definition in §§3.1 and 3.3 of the revised manuscript. The full construction and boundary handling are given in Appendix B. All reported experiments use this fixed encoding. We have not conducted a separate sensitivity study of the kernel width or encoding design.

The reference scores pˉa\bar p_a and pˉr\bar p_r correspond to the event reference state and, for regression and recovery, the recovery reference state. We first average comparison penalties within each event, then average over the valid sampled events in a batch. Why do Relation-only predictions collapse?

Critical Weaknesses · 11
The interesting finding that Relation-only predictions "collapse to nearly identical values close to zero across trajectory stages" is reported but not explained; an analysis of why relation-only supervision degenerates would strengthen the complementarity claim.
Questions For Authors · 3
Why do Relation-only progress predictions collapse to near-zero values? Can the authors provide more analysis or intuition for this degenerate behavior?

Response

We first use an example to intuitively explain why progress predictions at different task stages may shrink together when trained with relation supervision alone. For a no-further-progress event, progress at the later state should not exceed progress at the reference state, with penalty

ℓe(t)=[pt−p¯a]+2.\ell_e(t)=[p_t-\bar p_a]_+^2.

Predictions (p¯a,pt)=(0.4,0.6)(\bar p_a,p_t)=(0.4,0.6) incorrectly indicate increasing progress and incur a penalty of 0.040.04. Scaling them down to (0.04,0.06)(0.04,0.06) reduces the penalty to 0.00040.0004, while the predicted increase remains incorrect. Thus, for this type of relation penalty, the model can reduce the loss by shrinking prediction differences without learning the correct progress relation.

This also explains why relative relation supervision needs to be combined with absolute progress supervision. Relation supervision constrains which states should have higher or lower progress, while temporal supervision from successful demonstrations provides progress targets from 0 to 1 across task stages. When the model reduces the relation loss by lowering its predictions overall, these targets guide the predictions back toward their corresponding values, helping preserve progress differences across stages.

We have also added training diagnostics and controlled experiments to further examine this explanation. Given the length of the full experimental settings, results, and analysis, we present them in Appendix C.1.2 of the revised paper, under “Understanding Relation-only collapse.”

Response

We report online policy-learning time separately from preparation costs. In our plug-insertion experiment, Event2Reward reaches the success criterion after approximately 15 minutes of online training, compared with 45 minutes for Sparse. Both times include initial experience collection and robot resets during the online stage. Before training, the reward model is adapted using five successful demonstrations and five suboptimal trajectories. Progress labels for the successful demonstrations are generated automatically from time, so only the five suboptimal trajectories require event annotation, which took approximately one minute. Reward-model adaptation took approximately nine minutes on a single H200 GPU. The successful demonstrations were collected through human teleoperation, while the suboptimal trajectories were reused from recordings of previous policy runs. Figure R7 shows this training run with Event2Reward reward shaping: the first 10 trials are a human-guided warm-up, followed by autonomous policy exploration. The policy achieves 10 consecutive successful plug insertions at 15 min 16 s.

To illustrate the event-annotation process, we provide a continuous screen recording covering all 87 complete episodes from the paper’s Ethernet-insertion run Figure R8. One author familiar with the event definitions completed the annotations in 9 minutes 53 seconds of active work, excluding pauses, averaging 6.8 seconds per trajectory.

Constructing the Event2Reward dataset requires a one-time investment in model calls and human annotation. As an example, for the publicly released RoboChallenge data used in our paper, we used GPT-5.6 Sol (medium reasoning) to propose candidate event annotations. Annotating its main batch consumed approximately 3.8 billion tokens, with an estimated API cost of US$15,000–19,000. Human annotators independently annotate visible state changes in the videos without seeing the model outputs. Their annotations are then compared with the model proposals. Disagreements are reviewed by a third party, and annotation quality is checked through spot checks. Annotators only need to identify event types and mark key time points, without assigning framewise progress values. The initial independent annotation rate is approximately 260–330 trajectories per person-hour, or about 11–14 seconds per trajectory.

We will release the final event annotations, annotation protocol, and GPT prompts. Researchers can follow the protocol to carry out independent human annotation and quality review, or directly use the released final annotations to train reward models and reproduce the policy-learning experiments.
